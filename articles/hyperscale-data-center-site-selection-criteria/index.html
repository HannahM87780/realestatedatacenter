Hyperscale Data Center Site Selection Criteria: Power First, Then Everything Else

November 24, 2026

When a landowner calls me about a hyperscale buyer, the first question is usually about price per acre. It is the wrong first question. The buyer has already asked a different one, in a different order, and the site either passed or it did not. I appraise data center land, and site selection is where value is created or destroyed before a comp is ever pulled. In 2026 the order is not subtle: power first, then community support, then latency and proximity to customers, then everything else. A site that fails the first test does not get rescued by a good score on the rest.

The order, stated plainly by the people who study it

JLL says it directly in its 2026 outlook: speed to power is the primary criteria driving site selection, followed by community support, latency and proximity to customers. That ordering would have surprised a site selector fifteen years ago, when fiber routes and cheap land near a metro often led the screen and power was a utility call you made after. The reason for the flip is timing. JLL puts the average wait time for a grid connection in primary data center markets at more than four years. A building can be built faster than its power can arrive. So the screen now starts with deliverable megawatts and an energization date, in writing, from the utility.

Cushman and Wakefield scores the same reality at market level. Its 2026 Global Data Center Market Comparison evaluates 107 global markets across 24 key variables, grouped around operational IT load, power availability and delivery, grid stability, and the pipeline and preleasing picture. On its global fundamentals ranking, Virginia ranks first among primary markets, followed by Atlanta, Dallas, Columbus and Phoenix. That list is not a land price list. It is a deliverability list, and markets rise or fall on whether power, land, and approvals can line up on a schedule a tenant can finance.

Gate one: power capacity, voltage, and date

Treat power as a pass or fail gate, not a scored preference. The questions I want answered before I value a site as data center land are narrow: how many megawatts will the utility commit, at what voltage, from which substation, in what phases, and on what date. Large campuses typically need transmission level service, and the distance to a suitable line or substation is part of the economics, because a long dedicated build adds cost and time that the land price has to absorb.

The pipeline figures show why buyers are so strict. In one utility footprint tracked in federal permitting research, 60 GW of potential large load projects were in planning, with only 13 GW in advanced stages, and a 6 GW generation shortfall was estimated if all of them came online. Most announced load will never be built. The sites that trade at data center prices are the ones that move from announced to advanced, with a credible path through study, upgrades, and construction. That is the same distinction I made when I valued land by the megawatt: entitled power, not acreage, is the unit that matters. My piece on how to value data center land walks through that math, and the powered land piece explains why a megawatt of deliverable capacity carries the premium it does.

Power cost and reliability sit inside the same gate. Cushman and Wakefield added grid stability as a variable in 2026, measured by transmission and distribution losses. Those losses average 3.7% in the Americas, compared with 7.5% in EMEA and 8.2% in APAC, with Singapore lowest at 0.2%. A weak grid does not just risk outages. It pushes an operator toward larger backup systems and on site generation, which changes both the budget and the site plan. That is also why so many buyers now test a behind the meter option in parallel. I covered that shift in the behind the meter piece: on site generation is no longer exotic, it is a schedule hedge.

Gate two: land that can actually be built, and used by right

Pass the power gate and land becomes the next gate, not a simple acreage count. A hyperscale campus needs contiguous, buildable acres with room for buildings, substations, generators, setbacks, and later phases, outside the mapped floodplain for its core area, with title and assembly that can actually close. A typical new footprint in executive surveys runs 50 to 250 MW, which at the planning densities developers use means a site has to work at campus scale, not as a single building pad squeezed onto a commercial lot.

Zoning is part of this gate. By right use is worth real money because a discretionary rezoning adds a public hearing, a political vote, and a timeline the buyer does not control. I translate that into value the same way I do in my underwriting checklist: entitlement risk is a discount, and by right certainty is a premium, and confusing the two is how landowners get quoted a data center price for a site that is still only a hope. If you want the per acre side of that spread, the land cost per acre piece shows closed sales from farmland prices to powered prices in the core markets, and the gap is mostly this gate and the one before it.

Gate three: fiber, latency, and the workload split

Fiber still matters, it just no longer leads for every workload. A site needs diverse long haul routes, ideally entering from different directions, carrier access, and a practical path to splice points and exchanges. A single route with no diversity is a weakness a tenant will price or reject.

Latency depends on what will run inside. Training workloads exchange most of their data inside the campus and can tolerate a remote, power rich site. Inference, enterprise, and interconnection workloads serve users in real time and pull back toward metros. JLL expects that split to matter more soon: the sector will add 97 GW between 2025 and 2030, effectively doubling global capacity to about 200 GW, and inference is expected to overtake training as the dominant AI requirement in 2027. Read that as a site selector would: remote power rich land wins training today, and metro edge sites with strong fiber regain leverage as inference grows. One checklist does not fit both, and a buyer who tells you which workload is planned is telling you how to weight this gate.

The scored criteria: water, climate, incentives, and cost

Sites that pass the gates get ranked on the rest. Water and cooling are linked: the supply has to fit the cooling design, and a water stressed location pushes a project toward closed loop or air based systems that change cost and efficiency. That is why the PUE and WUE work belongs in a site screen, not just in operations. Climate, flood, seismic, wind, and wildfire exposure go on the same page, because insurers and lenders now ask for them by name.

Tax incentives can decide between two sites that both pass, because equipment is refreshed on a short cycle and sales tax on that equipment adds up. They rarely rescue a site that fails on power or zoning. I keep that order in the tax incentives scorecard: 38 states compete, but the incentive is a ranking factor, not a gate pass. Construction cost finishes the economics. All in greenfield cost averages $17.6 million per MW for the most modern facilities, with power infrastructure the single largest category at 21% of total cost. A market that is cheaper to build in helps, but only after the site can be powered, built, and approved. My construction cost piece breaks out what that figure includes and what it leaves out.

The criterion buyers used to treat as public relations: community support

JLL ranks community support second, ahead of latency, and the newest cancellation data shows why. Relae's tracker counted 70 U.S. data center projects blocked, withdrawn or stalled since January 2024, representing about $271 billion in announced or estimated investment. In the third quarter of 2026 alone, 16 projects were stopped, representing about $64 billion, and 11 of those 16 were stopped directly by a formal denial or a zoning ban. In the cases Relae analyzed in depth, transparency and process was the leading theme in 19 of 44 cases, ahead of water, which was the leading theme in only one.

For an appraiser, that is not a public relations footnote. It is entitlement risk with a dollar figure on it. A site in a jurisdiction with a moratorium, a hostile hearing record, or a pattern of denials is not the same site as an identical parcel one county over, even if the power and fiber match. I now ask about local precedent the way I ask about flood maps: early, in writing, and before I let a data center price into a valuation.

How I use this as an appraiser

I screen in the buyer's order and I value in that same order. First, is there a documented power path at the size and date the project needs. Second, can the land be built and used by right, or is approval still a vote away. Third, does the fiber and latency fit the stated workload. Only then do I rank water, incentives, climate risk, and build cost, and only then do I reach for comparable sales. A comp from a powered, entitled site does not support the value of an unpowered, unentitled one without large, explained adjustments, and in many cases it does not support it at all. That discipline is the point of the site selection screen: it keeps a hope from being priced as a campus.

The honest gaps

I want to be plain about what no checklist can settle. Utilities do not publish a single, comparable table of deliverable megawatts and energization dates by site, so the most important fact in the screen is usually a private utility letter, not a public database. Fiber maps are partly proprietary, incentive deals are often negotiated before they are public, and community sentiment cannot be read off a zoning map. There is no public score that ranks individual parcels the way Cushman and Wakefield ranks markets. Anyone selling a site with a single score and no utility letter, no zoning confirmation, and no fiber map is selling a summary, not evidence.

The bottom line

Hyperscale site selection in 2026 is a sequence, not a wish list. Power capacity and delivery date gate the deal, at a moment when a grid connection in a primary market takes more than four years on average. Buildable, by right land gates it next. Fiber and latency fit the workload. Water, incentives, and build cost rank the survivors. And community support, which JLL now places second, can stop a project outright, as $64 billion of projects stopped in one quarter shows. If you own land and a buyer is circling, get the answers in that order, in writing. The price conversation comes last, and it will be better for everyone if it does.
