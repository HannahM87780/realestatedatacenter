Data Center REIT vs. Direct Ownership Investing: Liquidity, Control, and the Price of Each

November 17, 2026

Investors keep asking me the same question in two different forms. Should I buy shares in a data center REIT, or should I try to own a piece of an actual building? I appraise these facilities for a living, and I understand why the question is hard. Both routes claim exposure to the same growth story. They do not buy you the same thing. A REIT share buys you liquidity, diversification, and a manager you cannot fire on your own schedule. Direct ownership buys you control, concentration, and a set of problems that are yours alone to solve. The right answer depends on which of those trades you actually want to make.

What a REIT share actually buys

A data center REIT owns a portfolio, not a building you can point to. Digital Realty, one of the two large listed names in this sector, reported 311 data centers in its portfolio as of September 30, 2026, holding about 3.2 gigawatts of IT capacity, with another 9.8 gigawatts of buildable capacity under active development or held for future development. That last figure is not operating capacity, and I want to be clear about that distinction. Equinix, the other large listed name, reported third quarter 2026 revenue of $2.571 billion, up 11% year over year, and raised its full year revenue guidance to $10.325 billion to $10.365 billion. When you buy either one, you are buying a slice of hundreds of leases, power contracts, and markets at once.

The structure forces cash out the door. Under federal tax law, a REIT must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income each year to keep its REIT treatment. That rule is why REIT investors see regular dividends, and it is also why REITs must keep returning to capital markets to fund growth. They cannot simply retain most of their earnings and build the next campus in silence.

Pricing is where the REIT route gets strange for a real estate person. Data center REITs have traded at an implied cap rate of 4.4%, the lowest of any major property sector in that survey, while actual U.S. data center transactions in the same broad period priced at a trailing cap rate of 6.9% on $29.8 billion of volume. Those two figures come from different sources and different dates, so I am not presenting them as a clean spread on the same day. I am presenting them as a warning I give in valuation work all the time: the public market price for a share and the private market price for a building are set by different buyers, on different evidence, and they can disagree for a long time.

What direct ownership actually buys

Direct ownership, whether that means a whole facility, a joint venture share, or a development position, starts at a completely different scale. In the return framework I use on this site, core stabilized strategies target 7% to 9% returns on tickets of $100 million or more, with 7 to 10 year holds. Development joint ventures target 15% to 20% returns on tickets of $50 million or more, with 3 to 5 year builds. Value add retrofit strategies sit between them at 12% to 15% returns on tickets of $30 million or more. Even the smallest of those tickets rules out most individual investors acting alone, which is why direct exposure usually arrives through a fund, a joint venture, or a syndication, with a manager between you and the building anyway.

What direct ownership gives you that a REIT cannot is say. You can choose the market, the tenant, the lease term, and the moment to sell or refinance. You also own the downside in concentrated form. Global occupancy was 97% at the end of 2025, and 93% of holders in one executive survey said their data center holding periods will exceed 5 years. This is a long hold asset class by design. If your one building loses its tenant, faces a power upgrade it cannot fund, or sits in a market where a new tariff lands, there is no portfolio of 310 other buildings to soften it. I covered how that concentration shows up in pricing in the sale leaseback piece, where the lease, not the deed, sets the price.

The income underneath both routes is the same rent, priced the same way. Hyperscale wholesale pricing runs $100 to $150 per kilowatt per month on 10 to 15 year terms, colocation at $150 to $250 per kilowatt per month on 5 to 10 year terms, and retail colocation at $200 to $400 per kilowatt per month on 1 to 3 year terms. A REIT blends all three tiers across hundreds of tenants. A direct owner lives with whichever tier, and whichever tenant, he actually signed. Digital Realty reporting an 11.8% cash increase on renewal rates and a $2.5 billion backlog of signed but not yet started leases at 100% share in the third quarter of 2026 tells you the blended portfolio is renewing well right now. It tells you nothing about whether one specific building in one specific market will do the same.

Liquidity is not a footnote. It is the main difference.

You can sell REIT shares during market hours. You cannot sell a data center that way. A direct position exits through a brokered sale, a refinancing, or a negotiated buyout, and each of those takes months and depends on a buyer who agrees with your cap rate. That illiquidity is not automatically bad. It protects a direct owner from daily repricing, and it forces the long hold this asset class rewards. But it changes what the investment is. A REIT position is a portfolio allocation you can rebalance. A direct position is a commitment you underwrite once and then live with, much like the staged commitment I described in how data centers are financed, where each layer of capital is priced for the risk of its stage.

The cost side cuts the other way. A direct owner faces the full construction and operating reality I laid out in the construction cost breakdown: a modern greenfield facility runs about $17.6 million per megawatt all in, before tenant IT fit out. A REIT shareholder faces that cost only as a diluted line in a development pipeline, funded by a professional team. If you do not have the staff to judge power procurement, cooling retrofits, and lease roll, and in my checklist for underwriting a data center those are the first questions, then direct control is control over decisions you may not be equipped to make.

Who each route suits, in my view

Buy the REIT if you want data center income, daily liquidity, and diversification across tenants and markets, and if you can accept that the share price will move with interest rates and equity sentiment, sometimes well away from the value of the buildings underneath. Buy direct, usually through a vehicle with real operating expertise, if you have the capital for a $30 million plus ticket at the low end of the institutional framework, you want a say over asset decisions, and you can hold for 5 to 10 years without needing the money back. Neither route is a shortcut around underwriting. The REIT investor still has to judge leverage, lease roll, and development exposure in the filings. The direct investor has to judge all of that for one asset, with no diversification to hide a mistake.

The honest gaps

I want to be plain about what I could not verify for this comparison. There is no public, like for like return series that puts listed data center REIT total returns and private direct ownership returns on the same basis, over the same period, with the same fees and leverage, in the sources I checked. Private direct returns are reported by managers, on their own definitions, and they are not audited the way a listed REIT filing is. I also could not verify a current average dividend yield for the sector that I would trust enough to print, because yields move with share prices every day and any figure I published this morning would be stale by next week. Anyone who gives you a single clean winner, with one return number for each side, is smoothing over those gaps rather than disclosing them.

The bottom line

A REIT and a direct stake are two different instruments that happen to rest on the same buildings. The REIT gives you 311 buildings at one manager alone, a legal duty to pay out at least 90% of taxable income, and an exit any trading day, at a public price that has implied a 4.4% cap rate while private deals priced near 6.9%. Direct ownership gives you control and concentration, at institutional ticket sizes, with target returns from 7% to 9% for core holds to 15% to 20% for development, and an exit measured in months or years. Choose based on liquidity, control, and ticket size first. The growth story is the same either way, and it is the one part of this decision you do not get to diversify.
