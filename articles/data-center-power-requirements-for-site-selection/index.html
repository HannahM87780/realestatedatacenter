Data Center Power Requirements for Site Selection: The Numbers a Site Has to Hit

November 26, 2026

In my last piece I laid out the order of the site selection screen: power first, then everything else. This piece is the numbers version of that first gate. When a developer tells me a site works, I do not ask whether it is near power. I ask how many megawatts, on which basis, under what contract terms, and by what date. Those are different questions, and in 2026 each one has a figure attached that can kill a site or carry it. I appraise data center land, and the power requirement is where a valuation either starts with evidence or starts with hope.

First, fix the basis: IT load is not the number the utility serves

Every power conversation goes wrong at the same place. A project described as 100 MW usually means 100 MW of IT load, the power the servers, storage, and network gear draw. The utility has to serve the whole facility: IT load plus cooling, power conversion losses, pumps, lighting, and everything else that keeps the white space running. That relationship is PUE, power usage effectiveness, and the math is simple multiplication. Total facility power equals IT load times PUE.

Run it at the current industry average. The Uptime Institute 2026 survey puts the industry average PUE at 1.52. So a 100 MW IT load needs 152 MW of facility power before redundancy and future phases are even discussed. The same building designed to a 1.3 PUE needs 130 MW. That 22 MW gap, which is arithmetic from the two PUE figures and not a market statistic, is a substation bay, a feeder, and sometimes a year of queue time. It is also why I covered PUE as an investment question in the PUE and WUE piece: efficiency is not an operating footnote, it is a site requirement. A site that can deliver 130 MW and not 152 MW works for one design and fails for the other.

Density is pushing the requirement up from inside the building. Average rack density hit 27 kW per rack in 2026, up from 16 kW a year earlier and 7 kW in 2021, and AI training facilities now demand 40 to 100 kW or more per rack. The floor area did not change. The power per square foot multiplied. When I screen a site, I ask for the planned density in writing, because a campus drawn for conventional racks and a campus drawn for AI racks are two different power requirements wearing the same site plan.

How much power a site has to offer, by the size of the prize

There is no single minimum megawatt figure for a data center site, and anyone who gives you one without asking about the tenant is guessing. What the market does have is a working scale. Executive surveys put a typical new footprint at 50 to 250 MW. Below that range a site can still work for enterprise or edge demand, but it is not competing for the hyperscale campuses that set land prices. At the top of the range and beyond it, the requirement stops being a distribution question and becomes a transmission question: dedicated substations, high voltage service, and a utility plan of service, not a tap on a local line.

Regulators have started writing their own thresholds into law and tariffs, and those thresholds tell you where a load stops being a customer and starts being a system event. In Texas, Senate Bill 6 gives the state utility commission review authority over co-location arrangements for large users of 75 MW or more. In Ohio, AEP Ohio's data center tariff applies its full process at 25,000 kW, which is 25 MW, and aggregates affiliated loads at one location to stop a big project from filing as several small ones. Read those two numbers together: somewhere between 25 MW and 75 MW, depending on the state, a data center leaves the normal service queue and enters a regime of studies, collateral, and minimum bills. A site pitched for a 100 MW campus has to be underwritten in that regime from day one.

The demand behind those thresholds is not slowing. U.S. data center electricity use is projected at 325 to 580 TWh by 2028, which is 6.7% to 12% of national consumption, implying 74 to 132 GW of total data center power demand. That range is wide, and I treat it as a range, not a forecast to build a single site on. But even the low end explains why utilities now ration deliverable megawatts the way lenders ration credit.

The queue is a requirement too: time, studies, and attrition

A site does not meet its power requirement when a developer files a request. It meets it when power is contracted and energized. JLL puts the average wait for a grid connection in primary data center markets at more than four years, which is why speed to power leads the criteria list in the site selection piece. The generation side of the same grid shows how structural the wait is. Berkeley Lab's Queued Up 2026 edition counted about 8,200 generation and storage projects in the transmission queue at the end of 2025, representing 1,312 GW of generation and about 749 GW of storage, and found the median time from interconnection request to commercial operation was over five years for projects built in 2025. I want to be precise about that source: Berkeley Lab's queue covers generation and storage, and it states that load requests are not included. It is evidence about the grid the load must connect to, not a load queue count.

The same Berkeley Lab work carries the attrition warning every land buyer should memorize: only 13% of the capacity that requested interconnection from 2000 to 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025, and 75% had been withdrawn. Queues are not pipelines. They are option books, and most options expire unexercised.

Texas shows the load side at full scale. ERCOT told its board in April 2026 that it was tracking approximately 410 GW of large load requests, about 87% of them data centers, after 198 GW of new requests arrived in the first quarter of 2026 alone. That 410 GW is requests, not contracted load, and ERCOT itself is moving to batch studies to sort real projects from placeholders. For a site screen, the lesson is direct: a queue position is worth something only when it survives study, and the megawatts in a press release are not the megawatts in a service agreement. That is the same discipline I apply when I value land by the megawatt method: powered land earns its premium, at $584,000 per MW in primary markets, only when the power is deliverable, and the powered land piece explains why the market pays for that certainty.

The contract is part of the requirement: ramps, minimums, and collateral

The newest change in site selection is that meeting the power requirement now means signing for it, on terms that punish speculation. AEP Ohio's tariff is the clearest published example, and I use it in screens because it is specific. A new load of 25 MW or more pays a one time load study fee of $10,000, $50,000, or $100,000, stepping up at 50 MW and 100 MW. The load ramp is capped at four years, with contract capacity of no less than 50% in year one, 65% in year two, 80% in year three, and 90% in year four. The initial term is the ramp plus eight years, so a four year ramp means a twelve year commitment. Monthly minimum billing demand is capped at 85% of total contract capacity, which in plain terms means the customer pays for most of the power it reserved whether it uses it or not. A customer that does not meet the tariff's credit and cash tests posts collateral equal to 50% of the total minimum charges for the full term.

Texas wrote a different version of the same idea for co-located loads. Under Senate Bill 6, the two arrangements approved by May 2026, a 265 MW data center paired with a 265.5 MW wind farm in Armstrong County and a 760 MW data center paired with a 1,099 MW natural gas plant in Freestone County, carry conditions that the load must cut use or switch to backup power, and the plant must return its full output to the grid, within 30 minutes of an ERCOT instruction. A behind the meter plan is therefore not an escape from grid obligations, as I noted in the behind the meter piece. It is a contract with the grid operator attached.

For site selection, these terms change which sites win. A site is no longer just acreage plus a substation. It is acreage plus a study fee, a ramp schedule the tenant's build out can actually hit, a minimum bill the project can carry through lease up, and collateral capacity on the sponsor's balance sheet. Power infrastructure is already the largest single slice of development cost, at 21% of the $17.6 million per MW all in greenfield figure, as the construction cost piece breaks out. The tariff terms add a second, contractual cost of power on top of the physical one.

The quantitative screen I actually run

Here is the checklist, in the order I run it, with the figure I need at each step. One: state the requirement on both bases, IT load and facility load at the design PUE, and write both numbers down. Two: confirm the utility can commit that facility figure, at transmission or distribution voltage as the size requires, from a named substation, in named phases. Three: get the energization date and test it against the queue reality, more than four years in primary markets, not against the developer's construction schedule. Four: price the contract, not just the rate: study fees, the ramp percentages, the minimum demand percentage, the term, the exit fee, and the collateral. Five: check the regulatory threshold the load crosses, 25 MW in AEP Ohio territory, 75 MW for Texas co-location review, and your own utility's equivalent, because crossing it changes the process. Six: only then compare sites, and compare them per deliverable megawatt, which is the comparison my underwriting checklist is built around.

The honest gaps

I want to be plain about what these numbers cannot do. There is no public, comparable table of deliverable megawatts and energization dates by site or even by substation, so the decisive fact in most screens is still a private utility letter. Queue totals mix real projects with duplicates and placeholders, and no source I trust separates them in real time; ERCOT's 410 GW and Berkeley Lab's 2,061 GW of generation and storage are context, not site evidence. Tariff terms also vary by utility and are changing fast in 2026, so the Ohio and Texas terms above are worked examples, not national rules. And no public source I found reports a verified average interconnection cost per MW for large loads, so I have not printed one. A screen that admits those gaps beats a screen that fills them with a sales deck.

The bottom line

A data center site meets its power requirement when four numbers line up: the facility megawatts, after PUE, that the design actually needs; a utility commitment for those megawatts at a named substation and date; a queue position that survives study in a system where most requests never get built; and a contract whose ramp, minimum bill, and collateral the project can carry. In 2026 that means thinking in 50 to 250 MW footprints, four plus year waits, 85% minimum bills, and thresholds at 25 MW and 75 MW that change the rules mid screen. Get those numbers in writing before you talk about price per acre. The land is only worth a data center price when the power requirement is met on paper, not just on a map.
