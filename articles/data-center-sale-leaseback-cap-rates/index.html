Data Center Sale Leaseback Cap Rates: Why the Rent Sets the Price

November 10, 2026

A sale leaseback in this business is simple to describe and easy to misread. An owner sells its data center to an investor and leases it back, usually the whole building on a long triple net lease, and keeps operating as the tenant. The seller gets capital out of the real estate. The buyer gets a long lease and a yield. When I am handed one of these deals as a comparable sale, my first reaction is not to write down the price. It is to ask what rent the seller agreed to pay to get that price, because in a sale leaseback the rent sets the price, and anyone who uses the price without the lease is only reading half the transaction.

The two kinds of sale leaseback, and why only one is priced on a cap rate

Data Center Dynamics, working with Cushman and Wakefield's data center advisory group, splits these deals into two kinds. The first is the 100 percent single tenant triple net leaseback, where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance on top of rent and stays for the long term. That is the kind investors price as a math equation: net operating income divided by a cap rate. The second is the vacant to short term leaseback, where the seller is on its way out to colocation or the cloud and leases back part of the building for a short period while it migrates. Buyers do not price that second kind on the leaseback rent. They price it on what the building earns with the next tenant, which is a very different question.

The seller's motive in the first kind is usually capital and focus, not distress. Cushman and Wakefield puts the cost of operating your own data center at typically 2 to 5 times the cost of leasing from a colocation provider, because a large operator shares engineers, security, and critical systems across many sites. Add the capital cycle Greenwood describes, generators and cooling and uninterruptible power systems coming due every few years for a company whose real business is banking or insurance or retail, and selling the building while staying as tenant starts to look like discipline rather than retreat. Whether it is discipline depends entirely on the rent and the term the seller signs to get the check.

What the cap rates actually look like

Start with the wider market, because a leaseback rate means nothing without it. MSCI put the U.S. data center trailing twelve month cap rate at 6.9% on $29.8 billion of volume in April 2026. That is the average transaction backdrop, across leased sales of all kinds. A long lease to a strong tenant should price tighter than that average. A short lease, a weak tenant, or a building that is hard to relet should price wider. When a leaseback prints far from that anchor, the lease is usually the reason.

The clearest current example of lease driven pricing is not a classic enterprise leaseback, and I label it that way on purpose. In June 2026 Digital Realty agreed to buy out Blackstone's interests in three fully leased Northern Virginia data centers, 288 megawatts in total, at a $7.8 billion gross value and an expected initial stabilized cap rate of over 6.5%. The income behind that price is 15 year leases to three investment grade hyperscale customers, with a blended average AA- credit rating and 3.6% annual rent escalators. Read that deal correctly and the building is almost incidental. What traded at over 6.5% was fifteen years of AA- rent growing at 3.6% a year in the largest data center market in the world. That is the top of the credit and term scale, and it is the right anchor for what a long, strong lease is worth.

Now take a true leaseback element. In January 2022 CyrusOne sold four Houston data centers to DataBank for about $670 million, on annualized cash net operating income of $34.8 million, for a transaction cap rate of 5.19%, and CyrusOne leased back the Houston West III shell to support a hyperscale lease it had just signed. Two lessons sit in that one deal. First, 5.19% was a price from a different rate world, struck before the rate reset that followed in 2022, and it cannot be carried into a 2026 valuation without a time adjustment. Second, part of the income the buyer priced included the seller's own future lease payments. That is normal in these structures. It is also exactly why the price needs to be unpacked before it is used as evidence of what the real estate alone would sell for.

Rates have moved enough to break old comparisons. Greenwood told Data Center Dynamics that triple net data center leasebacks could previously be bought at cap rates of 4.5% to 6.5% at the height of the market, and that the range can now run as high as 8.5% after public markets fell and interest rates climbed. A current industry guide points the same direction in more detail, putting hyperscale single tenant facilities on leases of 15 years or longer at 5.0% to 6.0%, stabilized multi tenant colocation at 5.5% to 7.0%, powered shell at 6.5% to 8.0%, and edge and secondary markets at 7.0% to 9.0%, with investment grade hyperscaler leases trading 100 to 200 basis points tighter than non rated tenants. I treat that guide as a range to test against, not as a survey, because no public survey of these deals exists. The pattern across all three sources is consistent, though: term and tenant credit move the rate by points, not by tenths.

Why these sales mislead assessors and comp users

Altus Group's Nicholas Carter made the point I wish every assessor and every analyst would tape to the wall. A sale leaseback price makes sense to the buyer and the seller, but analysts who use these transactions as a proxy for market value are often misled, because the two parties' interests run in parallel rather than against each other. If the seller wants a higher price, the seller can agree to pay a higher rent, and the higher rent, capitalized, produces the higher price. The deed records a real price. The lease that manufactured a part of that price often does not record at all.

Greenwood makes the same point from the pricing side. With a triple net leaseback, value follows from the cap rate and the net operating income, and there are two levers to move: if the tenant agrees to pay above or below market rent, the purchase price moves directly with it. So the test is never just the cap rate. The test is whether the contract rent is market rent. On this site's own source set, hyperscale wholesale pricing runs $100 to $150 per kilowatt per month on 10 to 15 year terms, colocation at $150 to $250 per kilowatt per month on 5 to 10 year terms, and retail colocation at $200 to $400 per kilowatt per month on 1 to 3 year terms. Those are broad 2026 tiers, not a rent survey for any one building, but they are enough to flag a leaseback rent that sits well above its tier. Above market rent capitalized at a market cap rate is not market value of the real estate. Part of it is a financing premium the seller will repay, with interest, through the lease.

Volume makes the problem worse. Greenwood reckoned there are on average about 12 sale leaseback transactions across the entire U.S. market each year. Twelve deals a year, in a sector this large, means every leaseback gets reused as a comparable far beyond what it can support, in markets it never traded in, for buildings it does not resemble. In the checklist I use to underwrite a data center, a leaseback comp gets an extra page of questions for exactly this reason.

How I handle a sale leaseback in valuation work

First, I separate the contract from the real estate. I value the building at market rent first, using the same discipline I use in the $/MW method for data center land: what would this power, this location, and this box earn from the next operator? Then I value the difference between contract rent and market rent for what it is, a leasehold premium or discount that lives and dies with that one tenant's credit and remaining term.

Second, I test the term against the building. A 15 year lease sounds long until you set it against cooling and power systems that will need major capital well inside that window, and against a construction cost base that now runs into the tens of millions of dollars per megawatt all in, as the construction cost breakdown on this site shows. Who pays for the mid lease capital, the tenant under a true triple net or the landlord by side agreement, changes the net income the cap rate is applied to. Gross looking rent with landlord capital hiding behind it is not the same income.

Third, I price the empty building. If the tenant leaves at expiry, or earlier, what is a single purpose, single tenant box worth vacant in that market, and how long does it sit? In a market where powered land itself trades at a scarcity premium, as I covered in the powered land piece, the land and the power position may carry the residual. In a secondary market with no second operator waiting, the residual can be a small fraction of the leaseback price. A cap rate that never asks that question is pricing a bond and calling it real estate.

The honest gaps

There is no public survey of data center sale leaseback cap rates, and I want to be plain about that rather than dress up the fragments. The figures in this post come from one MSCI market average, two disclosed transactions from different rate eras, a 2024 trade analysis, and a 2026 guide whose ranges I could not tie to a published transaction list. Most leaseback rents, escalators, and renewal options are private, and the deals that do disclose a cap rate rarely disclose whether the rent was at market. Anyone who gives you a single sale leaseback cap rate for 2026, without a tenant, a term, and a market attached, is giving you a slogan, not a statistic. The per acre and per megawatt cross checks I use for land, covered in the land cost per acre piece, exist because this sector's headline prices so often need a second unit to stay honest. Leaseback prices need the same treatment, with the lease itself as the second unit.

The bottom line

Data center sale leaseback cap rates in 2026 sit around a 6.9% market average, tighter for long leases to investment grade hyperscalers and wider, in some cases much wider, for short terms, powered shell, and secondary markets. But the rate is the least informative part of the deal. The rent, the term, the escalators, and the tenant's credit are what the price is actually made of, and a seller can move the price by moving the rent. Use a leaseback as a comparable only after you have tested its rent against market, separated the contract premium from the real estate, and priced the building empty. Until then, you do not have a comp. You have a financing wearing a deed.
