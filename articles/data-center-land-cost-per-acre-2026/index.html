Data Center Land Cost per Acre in 2026: From $4,500 Farmland to $6.33 Million in Loudoun

November 5, 2026

The question landowners ask me first is almost always the same one: what is an acre worth to a data center developer? It is a fair question and, on its own, a misleading one. Per acre is the unit farmers, brokers, and county officials think in, so it is the unit I have to answer in. But the honest answer in 2026 is a spread so wide it sounds made up, from a national farmland average in the low thousands per acre to a closed sale above $6 million per acre in Loudoun County, Virginia. This post lays out that spread with closed sales and published figures, and then explains what actually moves a parcel from one end of it to the other.

Start with the floor: what an acre is worth as farmland

The U.S. Department of Agriculture puts the national average value of farm real estate at $4,500 per acre in 2026, with cropland at $6,020 per acre and pasture at $2,000 per acre. That is the floor in most rural markets, the value of the land in its farm use before any data center buyer shows up. Keep that number in mind, because every figure that follows is a multiple of it, and the size of the multiple is the whole story.

A developer does not pay a multiple of farm value for soil. He pays it for position: position in the power queue, position relative to transmission, and position inside a market where tenants already want to be. Strip those away and the acre goes back to being worth roughly what the USDA says it is worth.

The middle of the market: six figures in farm country

In Pike County, Ohio, an SB Energy subsidiary closed on 781 acres for $86.7 million in 2026, about $111,000 per acre, according to Pike County Auditor records reported in October 2026. The county auditor had valued those same parcels at about $7.5 million in total. The closings priced the ground at more than eleven times its auditor value, for a campus next to the former Portsmouth Gaseous Diffusion Plant that is still working through its permits. That is the middle of this market in one deal: farmland, in a rural county, at $111,000 per acre, because a credible power and site plan sits behind it.

Avison Young makes the same point from the other direction. Land that might sell for roughly $10,000 per acre without a viable data center power path can become worth six figures per acre once a developer establishes the infrastructure and utility relationships a large project needs. Nothing about the dirt changed. The power path changed, and the price followed it. I made the same argument in my piece on how to value data center land: the premium attaches to deliverable megawatts, not to acres.

The top of the market: Northern Virginia

Northern Virginia is where per acre numbers stop sounding like farmland and start sounding like downtown. Three closed or board approved deals from the last year set the range.

Amazon Data Services bought the George Washington University campus in Ashburn, about 122 acres for $427.3 million, roughly $3.5 million per acre, in a sale announced in February 2026. In Fairfax County, the board of supervisors approved selling 41.7 acres of county land in Chantilly for $166.8 million, about $4 million per acre, to a Starwood affiliate for data center development. And in Loudoun County, records show a data center developer paid $615.05 million for 97.15 acres in Leesburg in November 2025, about $6.33 million per acre, for the Twin Creeks development.

Avison Young frames that top end cleanly: in Northern Virginia, entitled land without power trades at roughly $4 million per acre, compared with at least $6 million per acre for powered land. Read the three deals against that frame and they line up. The $3.5 million to $4 million deals are entitled, well located acres. The $6.33 million deal is what the market pays when power, entitlements, and assembly are already solved.

Two cautions belong here, and I will state them the way I would in a report. First, the Twin Creeks price does not isolate the value of electricity. The reporting on that sale is explicit that entitlements, development rights, site assembly, and market conditions are all inside the number. Second, these are the most competitive acres in the most competitive data center market in the world. Using a Loudoun price to value a field in Ohio is not an appraisal. It is a sales pitch.

Why power explains most of the spread

If you want one variable that predicts where a parcel lands in the spread, it is committed power. An Altus Group analysis of 80 verified data center land transactions found that committed utility load was the strongest predictor of land pricing, and sites tied to 230 kilovolt or 500 kilovolt infrastructure traded at roughly 2.4 times the pricing of sites tied to 138 kilovolt or lower infrastructure. Developers generally plan on about 0.5 to 1 acre per megawatt, according to Avison Young, which is the bridge between the per acre world and the per megawatt world.

Run that bridge against the powered land benchmark from the Cushman and Wakefield cost guide, $584,000 per megawatt for powered land in primary U.S. markets. At one acre per megawatt, that benchmark implies about $584,000 per acre. At half an acre per megawatt, it implies about $1.17 million per acre. That is arithmetic, not a new market statistic, and I label it that way because it matters: the national powered land average, converted honestly, sits far below the Loudoun closings and far above the Ohio closing. Per acre and per megawatt are two views of the same scarcity, and I trust the per megawatt view more, for the reasons I laid out in the powered land piece.

The demand behind all of this is not slowing. U.S. purchases of land for future data centers reached about $6 billion in the first half of 2026, up 79% from a year earlier, according to Avison Young. And for sites that cannot wait on the grid at all, the behind the meter structures I covered in my last post are becoming the other way to buy speed.

How to use a per acre number without fooling yourself

If a developer, a broker, or a neighbor gives you a per acre figure, ask three questions before you repeat it. Was it a closed sale, a board approved price, or an asking price and an option headline? Options and announcements routinely overstate what closes, because the headline price assumes power and entitlements that may never arrive. What came with the acres: entitlements, a utility commitment, site assembly, or raw ground and a hope? And what market is it actually in, because a per acre price is the least portable number in this business?

For valuation work, I use per acre as the cross check and dollars per deliverable megawatt as the primary unit, then I test the land price against the total project. A campus that costs $17.6 million per megawatt to build all in can carry a high land basis on the right site, which is why buyers stretch. It cannot carry any land basis on the wrong one, which is why so many announced sites quietly die. The construction cost breakdown on this site shows where that $17.6 million goes, and my underwriting checklist shows how I test whether a site survives it.

The honest gaps

There is no public database of verified data center land sales priced per acre, and there is not likely to be one soon. Virginia records gave us the Loudoun and Fairfax figures above because those deals ran through county records and a public board vote. In nondisclosure states like Texas, most prices never become public at all. Much of what circulates as a per acre price is an asking price, an option headline, or a number repeated from a community meeting. I have used closed sales and a board approved county sale in this post wherever I could, and labeled the market framing from Avison Young as framing, not as a deed. Where the record is thin, the right move is to say so, not to average the rumors into a false precision.

The bottom line

Data center land cost per acre in 2026 runs from a $4,500 national farmland average to $6.33 million per acre for a powered, entitled, assembled site in Loudoun County, with credible farm country closings like Pike County, Ohio, at about $111,000 per acre in between. The acre is not what is being priced. Power, entitlements, and certainty are being priced, and the acre is just the wrapper they arrive in. If someone quotes you a per acre number, ask what power comes with it, ask whether it closed, and ask which market it closed in. Those three answers tell you whether you are looking at a comp or at a story.
