Data Center Rent per kW per Month in 2026: Asking, Signed, and What Rent Leaves Out

November 19, 2026

The first number anyone quotes me on a data center lease is the rent per kilowatt per month. It sounds precise. It is not, until you know three things: whether it is an asking rent or a signed rent, what size requirement it was quoted for, and what it leaves out. I appraise these facilities, and rent is the income line every valuation starts from. Get the rent definition wrong and the value that follows will be wrong in the same direction. In 2026 the benchmarks are at record levels, and the gap between the headline average and what a specific tenant actually signs has rarely mattered more.

The benchmark: $204.69, and it depends on size

CBRE's average asking rent for wholesale colocation in primary North American markets reached $204.69 per kW per month for a 250 to 500 kW requirement in the first half of 2026, up 4.3% in six months. That is the most quoted figure in this market, and it is an asking rent, meaning what operators quote before negotiation, for one reference size, at N+1 or Tier III equivalent redundancy.

Size changes the price, even in a sold out market. On the same CBRE series, 500 kW to 3 MW requirements averaged $183.75 per kW per month, up 7.9% in the half, 3 to 10 MW averaged $175.00, up 8.3%, and 10 MW and up averaged $165.31, up 6.7%. The discount for taking a larger block still exists, at about 19% between the smallest and largest bands by my arithmetic on CBRE's figures. What changed in 2026 is that the large bands rose fastest. Contiguous multi megawatt blocks are the scarcest product in the market, so they are no longer discounted the way they once were.

Put the run in context. That same 250 to 500 kW average has risen about 69% since 2020, from $120.79 to $204.69 per kW per month, and CBRE expects it to exceed $215 per kW per month for 2026. An older figure in my files makes the same point from a different angle: requirements of 1 MW and above averaged 13.0% rent increases in 2025 as operators pushed pricing. This is not one hot quarter. It is a six year repricing of powered capacity.

Rents by market: where you lease matters as much as how much

The national average hides a wide spread. For 250 to 500 kW requirements in the first half of 2026, CBRE's asking bands were $190 to $235 per kW per month in Northern Virginia, $200 to $275 in Silicon Valley, $195 to $225 in Chicago, $175 to $215 in Phoenix, $185 to $200 in Atlanta, and $160 to $185 in Dallas-Fort Worth. In Northern Virginia, the largest market, a 10 MW and up requirement asked $160 to $185 per kW per month, below the small requirement band in the same market.

Two cautions belong with any market table. First, these are bands of asking rents, not averages and not signed deals, and a real quote can land outside them. Second, market bands reflect scarcity in that place at that moment, not just building quality. Silicon Valley's wide band and high top end sit next to the highest power costs in the primary set. Dallas-Fort Worth sits lower despite enormous construction, because more of its pipeline is in secondary style sites with a clearer power path. When I wrote about land cost per acre, committed utility load was the strongest predictor of land pricing. The same logic runs through rent: you are paying for deliverable power, not for square feet.

Asking is not signed, and signed is not one number either

Here is the distinction I wish every rent headline carried. Asking rent is an opening quote. Contract rent is what a tenant signs, after size, term, credit, timing, and ramp are negotiated. Effective rent is contract rent adjusted for free rent, allowances, and escalators across the full term, and it is the only fair basis for comparing two deals.

Signed figures from a public operator show how wide the signed range can be inside one company, one quarter, and one region. Digital Realty reported new lease pricing in the Americas in the second quarter of 2026 at $156 per kW per month for deployments above 1 MW and $293 per kW per month for deployments below 1 MW. That $293 figure is above the CBRE asking average, and the $156 figure is below the CBRE average for the largest band, and neither fact is an error. Small deployments pay for flexibility, shorter terms, and retail style services. Large deployments trade size and term for price. Comparing a signed small deployment rent to an asking large block average, without naming both bases, is how bad comps are born.

Renewals tell a related story. Digital Realty reported rental rates on renewal leases rising 11.8% on a cash basis in the third quarter of 2026. A renewal increase is not a new lease asking rent, but for an owner it is the closest evidence of what in place tenants will pay to stay, and for an appraiser it is a useful check on whether a market rent opinion is drifting below what leases are actually doing.

Why rents are rising: 1.4% vacancy and a preleased pipeline

Rent does not rise this fast without scarcity underneath it. Across CBRE's eight primary markets in the first half of 2026, vacancy was 1.4%, supply had grown to 10,903 MW, net absorption was about 1,456 MW, up 11.7%, and 80.4% of the 7,481 MW under construction was already preleased. Supply grew by about a third in a year and vacancy still fell. Less than 1,500 MW of everything being built remained uncommitted, roughly six months of demand at the current pace in the summaries I read.

That is why I treat the current rent level differently from a speculative spike. Tenants are signing years ahead of delivery, which is the behavior I described in the financing piece, where construction capital is committed against pre leasing, and in the REIT comparison, where a large backlog of signed but not yet started leases supports future income. Scarcity today is documented. Scarcity in 2029 is a forecast, and I do not underwrite a forecast the same way I underwrite a signed lease.

What rent leaves out

A rent per kW looks complete. It rarely is. In most wholesale leases electricity is billed on top, metered and passed through, often with a power usage effectiveness factor or adder, so two quotes at the same rent can produce very different total bills in markets with different power prices. That is one reason the PUE work matters to rent analysis: the efficiency factor is part of the tenant's real cost even when it never appears in the rent line.

Add the rest before comparing proposals: cross connects and carrier connections, remote hands beyond any included hours, fit out inside the space, taxes and operating costs under a net lease, and any security deposit or letter of credit, which ties up capital even when it is not a monthly cost. Lease structure moves the headline too. In the framework I use on this site, hyperscale wholesale pricing on long triple net style terms and colocation pricing on shorter terms have always been different products, at $100 to $150 per kW per month for hyperscale wholesale on 10 to 15 year terms, $150 to $250 for colocation on 5 to 10 year terms, and $200 to $400 for retail colocation on 1 to 3 year terms. A triple net tenant pays operating costs on top, so a lower rent there is not automatically a cheaper deal. Escalators finish the picture: a modest annual escalator, compounded over a 10 to 15 year term, moves total rent by millions on a multi megawatt lease, which is why I negotiate and underwrite the escalator as hard as the starting rate. I covered that math in the sale leaseback piece, where escalators and term are what turn rent into price.

How I use these figures in valuation

I start with the CBRE band for the subject's size and market, then I test it against signed evidence where I can find it, then I adjust for what the quoted rent includes. If the subject lease is triple net and the benchmark is a turnkey asking rent, they are not comparable until the operating load is reconciled. If the subject is a 500 kW requirement, I do not use the 10 MW average because it is the newest number. If the lease has above market escalators or a long ramp, I model effective rent across the term, not year one rent capitalized as if it were flat. That discipline is the core of my underwriting checklist, and rent is where it is tested first, because every later step, from cap rate selection to residual value, inherits the rent opinion.

The honest gaps

I want to be plain about the limits here. There is no public database of signed data center rents at lease level. CBRE's series is asking rents for reference sizes, published as averages and market bands, and operator disclosures are signed rents on an accounting basis that spreads escalators across the term, so the two series should not be blended into one average. Private lease terms, concessions, and effective rents are mostly not public, and no source I checked publishes a verified effective rent series after free rent and allowances. Anyone quoting you a single national rent, without size, market, and basis, is giving you a headline, not a comp.

The bottom line

In 2026, use $204.69 per kW per month only as what it is: CBRE's first half average asking rent for a 250 to 500 kW requirement in primary North American markets, up 4.3% in the half and about 69% since 2020. For a real underwriting decision, replace it with the band for your size, $183.75 at 500 kW to 3 MW, $175.00 at 3 to 10 MW, and $165.31 at 10 MW and up, then with the band for your market, then with signed evidence if you can get it, and then add back everything rent leaves out, starting with power. At 1.4% vacancy and with four fifths of the construction pipeline already preleased, operators hold the pricing power today. The appraiser's job is not to argue with that fact. It is to make sure the rent in the model is the rent the lease will actually pay.

Sources: CBRE, North America Data Center Trends H1 2026 (August 2026) and CBRE Midyear Review (August 2026); Digital Realty second and third quarter 2026 results; Data Center E-Primer (2026); OD6584, Hyperscale Data Center Services in the US Industry Report (March 2026); JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook.
