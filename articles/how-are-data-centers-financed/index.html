How Are Data Centers Financed: From Construction Loans to ABS

November 12, 2026

People outside this business picture a data center loan the way they picture a warehouse loan. A bank lends against a finished building, the tenant pays rent, and everyone goes home. That picture misses almost everything that makes these deals work. A data center is financed in stages, and each stage has a different lender, a different risk, and a different price. I underwrite these buildings for a living, and the financing stack is not background detail. It is the clearest map I have of who believes what about the project, because every layer of money in the stack is priced on a different bet.

Start with the scale, because scale explains the stack

The money required is too large for any one lender or any one structure. JLL estimates that up to $3 trillion will be required to support 100 gigawatts of new capacity, and that figure excludes the $1 trillion to $2 trillion tenants spend on their own IT fit out. At the top of the market, hyperscalers are committing $635 billion to $690 billion in capital spending in 2026 alone. A single campus can cost more than a regional bank wants on its books in one asset class. So the capital arrives in pieces, each piece matched to the risk that exists at that moment in the project's life, and each piece replaced by cheaper money as the risk falls.

Stage one: construction loans that behave like a partnership test

Construction debt is the cautious money. Senior construction loans typically fund 55% to 65% of costs, and they usually require about 60% of the project to be pre-leased before the lender is comfortable. Sit with those two numbers, because together they tell you how this sector actually gets built. The lender will not fund a speculative box on hope, and the developer cannot fill the gap with enthusiasm. Before the first loan dollar moves, a creditworthy tenant has usually committed to most of the building, and the developer and its equity partners are carrying 35% to 45% of the cost themselves.

That is why powered land and a signed power agreement matter so much before financing even starts, as I covered in the powered land piece. A lender's first questions are not about the roof. They are about whether the power is real, whether the tenant is real, and whether the lease term outlasts the loan by enough years to refinance. In the checklist I use to underwrite a data center, those three answers decide whether the rest of the numbers are worth reading.

Stage two: mezzanine and preferred equity fill the gap, at a price

Between the senior loan and the developer's own equity sits the expensive layer. Mezzanine debt and preferred equity typically fill 15% to 25% of the capital stack, price at 12% to 15% returns, and often carry a PIK toggle, which means part of the interest is paid in kind, added to the balance instead of paid in cash. That pricing is not greed. It is the market's honest read of the risk in that seat: behind the senior lender, ahead of the common equity, exposed to construction delays and lease up risk, and paid last if the schedule slips.

For an appraiser, this layer matters for a reason most people miss. When I see heavy mezzanine in a stack, I am looking at a project whose sponsor needed the deal to work at a cost of capital well above the rent the market was offering on day one. The building may still be excellent. But the financing is telling me the margin for error is thin, and thin margins show up later as deferred maintenance, aggressive renewal assumptions, or a sale price the seller needs rather than a price the market chose.

Stage three: stabilization, then the takeout

Once a facility is built, leased, and producing income, the expensive construction era money is meant to leave. It is replaced by permanent financing priced against cash flow. Typical advance rates run at 75% to 80% of cash flow, at 65% to 70% loan to value. The cap rate environment sets the value that loan to value is measured against, which is why the sale leaseback market I wrote about recently and the lending market are really one conversation. A building that appraises on strong, long, investment grade income can carry more and cheaper debt. A building on a short lease to a weak credit cannot, no matter how new the generators are.

The return targets explain who holds each stage. Core stabilized strategies target 7% to 9% returns on tickets of $100 million or more, with 7 to 10 year holds. Development joint ventures target 15% to 20% returns on tickets of $50 million or more, with 3 to 5 year builds. Same asset class, nearly opposite risk, and the spread between those two ranges is the price of taking a project from dirt and a power queue position to a leased, income producing facility.

Stage four: securitization turns buildings into bonds

The newest layer, and the one that has changed this sector's financing the most, is securitization. About $57 billion in U.S. data center securitization issuance has closed since 2021, including about $15 billion in asset backed securities and about $11 billion in 2025 alone. Sell side desks project $30 billion to $40 billion per year in gross securitized issuance ahead, and Morgan Stanley estimates about $130 billion in U.S. data center securitized credit financing needs by 2028, with about $28 billion in net issuance projected for 2026 alone.

Here is what that means in plain terms. Instead of one bank holding one loan on one building, the income from leased data centers is pooled, sliced, and sold to bond investors as asset backed securities and commercial mortgage backed securities. That brings in a much deeper pool of capital than the bank market alone can supply, and it lets developers recycle their equity into the next project instead of waiting a decade to get it back. It also changes the underwriting, because bond investors and rating agencies read leases the way appraisers do. In this site's own source set, CMBS underwriting flags lease roll of more than 30% within 24 months, and more than 40% within 12 months, as a risk signal. A portfolio where too much rent expires at once is hard to securitize, hard to refinance, and, I would add, hard to value at the price its owner wants.

The rent tiers underneath all of this are worth stating, because securitized income is only as good as the leases producing it. Hyperscale wholesale pricing runs $100 to $150 per kilowatt per month on 10 to 15 year terms, colocation at $150 to $250 per kilowatt per month on 5 to 10 year terms, and retail colocation at $200 to $400 per kilowatt per month on 1 to 3 year terms. Longer terms and larger blocks trade at lower monthly rates and finance more easily. Short, fragmented retail income pays more per kilowatt and worries a lender more. Price and financeability pull in opposite directions, and a good underwrite holds both facts at once.

What the stack tells an appraiser, and what it hides

I read a capital stack the way I read a rent roll: as a set of claims that need testing. Heavy construction debt with thin pre-leasing would be a warning, except the market's own norms, 55% to 65% of cost and about 60% pre-leased, exist because lenders learned to demand them. A large mezzanine layer at 12% to 15% tells me where the project's real risk premium sits, whatever the brochure cap rate says. A securitized takeout tells me a rating agency and a bond buyer have already stress tested the leases, which is useful evidence, though it is evidence about the income, not about what the empty building would sell for.

That last distinction is the one I keep coming back to in valuation work, the same one I raised with sale leaseback pricing. Financing values a contract: this tenant, this rent, this term, this credit. Market value has to survive the contract ending. The construction cost base now runs to $17.6 million per megawatt all in for a modern greenfield facility, as the construction cost breakdown on this site shows, and the land underneath it is priced by the megawatt, as the $/MW method for data center land explains. A financing that only works if the first tenant renews at the first rent is not proof of value. It is proof that a lender made the same assumption the borrower did.

The honest gaps

Public visibility into this stack is uneven, and I want to be plain about that. The securitization totals are tracked because rated deals are documented. Private construction loans, mezzanine terms, and joint venture promotes mostly are not. The figures in this post come largely from one industry primer's synthesis of KBRA, CreditSights, and Morgan Stanley material, which is a credible source set but still a synthesis, not a loan level database. I could not verify from public sources what share of current projects actually carry mezzanine debt, or what the average construction loan spread is in November 2026, so I have not guessed at either. Anyone who gives you one average cost of capital for data centers, without a stage, a tenant, and a term attached, is averaging things the market prices very differently.

The bottom line

Data centers are financed in stages because the risk changes in stages. Senior construction loans fund 55% to 65% of cost against about 60% pre-leasing. Mezzanine and preferred equity fill 15% to 25% of the stack at 12% to 15%. Stabilized buildings refinance at 65% to 70% loan to value, and securitization, $57 billion issued since 2021 and growing, turns the resulting income into bonds and recycles the equity into the next campus. Read the stack before you trust the price. Each layer is a priced opinion about the power, the tenant, and the lease, and the most expensive layer in the stack is usually the most honest one.

Sources: Data Center E-Primer (2026), citing KBRA, CreditSights, and Morgan Stanley; JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook; OD6584, Hyperscale Data Center Services in the US Industry Report (March 2026).
