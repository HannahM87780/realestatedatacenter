Data Center Tax Incentives by State: The 2026 Scorecard

October 29, 2026

For twenty years, the logic was simple. A data center brings a large investment, a small headcount, and a tax base that does not send children to local schools. States competed to land them with the tool they control most directly: they stopped charging sales tax on the equipment inside. When a single campus can fill a building with servers and cooling gear worth hundreds of millions of dollars, that exemption is not a ribbon cutting courtesy. It is a core part of the deal math, and for a long time almost nobody priced it in public.

In 2026, states started pricing it in public. The scorecard below is not a ranking of good states and bad states. It is a record of what changed this year: the count of programs still standing, the first honest cost figures from the biggest states, and a wave of pauses that tells you more about where site selection is headed than any new incentive announcement does.

How widespread this got before anyone added it up

At least 38 states offer data center tax incentives as of April 2026, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures. Kansas became the 38th state in July 2025. Every one of those 38 waives sales tax on essential data center equipment. 14 states extend the exemption to electricity, and 11 also offer a partial property tax abatement. The programs usually carry an investment floor and a jobs floor. Kansas, for example, asks for at least $250 million invested and 20 jobs created within two years of opening.

That structure explains both the appeal and the argument. The floors sound large until you set them next to the cost of a modern campus. The construction figures I track run from about $9.8 million to $13.3 million per MW in major markets, and up to $17.6 million per MW all in, before AI fit out. A project that clears a $250 million floor is not an unusual hyperscale project. It is a normal one. Which means the exemption was not really screening projects in or out in the biggest markets. It was lowering the cost of projects that were already being built at scale, and the public cost stayed quiet because in many states nobody was required to publish it.

Good Jobs First, which tracks subsidy disclosure, put a useful limit on the debate this year: 14 of the 37 states with sales and use tax exemptions do not publish their official revenue losses in a timely manner. Read any total you see, including the state figures below, with that gap in mind. The states that do publish are the states where the argument is happening. The others are not necessarily cheaper. They are just less visible.

Virginia: the largest program, and the clearest audit

Virginia built the model most states copied. Its retail sales and use tax exemption asks for $150 million in new capital investment and 50 new jobs, with each job paid at least 150% of the prevailing local average wage, and it runs through June 30, 2035. The state's own audit commission, JLARC, found the exemption provided $928 million in tax savings in fiscal 2023 and that about 90% of the industry in Virginia uses it.

Then the annual figure passed a billion. Virginia missed more than $1.6 billion in tax revenue in fiscal 2025 because of data center exemptions, up 118% on the prior year. Looked at over a longer window, JLARC found the exemption accounted for $2.7 billion, or 53% of all Virginia incentive spending, from fiscal 2015 to fiscal 2024. In the 2026 session the state Senate proposed phasing the exemption out starting January 1, 2027, well ahead of its 2035 expiration. The House rejected that approach, and as of June 2026 Virginia had not ended the program. That stalemate is itself the scorecard entry: the largest program in the country is now too large to leave alone and too embedded to repeal quickly.

For land, that matters more than the headline fight. Northern Virginia pricing, including the powered land premium I wrote about earlier this fall, was built in a market where this exemption was treated as permanent background. A phase out or a narrowing would not erase the power and fiber advantages that made the market. It would change the equipment economics inside the building, which flows into rents, which flows into value. Anyone valuing a Virginia site today who treats the 2035 date as a guarantee, rather than as a date politicians are actively trying to move, is underwriting a political assumption as if it were a physical fact.

Texas and Ohio: projections that missed by an order of magnitude

Texas runs a tiered version. A qualifying data center needs 100,000 square feet, 20 jobs, and $200 million invested over five years, and gets 10 to 15 years free of the 6.25% state sales tax on essential equipment, with local taxes still due. A large project, at 250,000 square feet, 40 jobs, and $500 million, gets 20 years. The Texas Comptroller figures reported this spring show why the program is now in question. The break cost more than $150 million in 2023 and at least $1.3 billion in 2026, the Comptroller forecast puts it at nearly $1.8 billion a year by fiscal 2030, and the two year cost was put at $3.2 billion. State leaders have talked openly about repeal or narrowing in the January 2027 legislative session. No repeal had been enacted in the sources I checked for this post, so Texas belongs in the at risk column, not the repealed column.

Ohio is the cleanest case of a forecast failure. Its program asks for $100 million invested over three consecutive years and at least $1.5 million in annual compensation at the site. The exemption cost $555 million in 2024 and nearly $1.6 billion in 2025, against an initial projection for 2025 of about $136 million. That is not a rounding error. That is a program whose cost ran about twelve times the estimate, in a single year, because the industry grew faster than the model. On May 27, 2026, Governor DeWine paused consideration of new requests while a legislative committee studies the issue. Existing agreements continue. His office also noted the other side of the ledger, that recipients invested $27.2 billion in Ohio in 2025. Both numbers belong in the same paragraph. A pause built on only the cost figure, or only the investment figure, is not an honest reading.

Georgia shows the same pattern at a slightly smaller scale, with a tiered floor that drops to $25 million and 5 jobs in counties under 30,000 people, compared with $250 million and 25 jobs in counties over 50,000. The state's own audit put the state revenue cost at $474 million in 2025, with a state only projection of $625 million in 2026. I am labeling that as state only on purpose. Figures that add local losses run much higher, and mixing the two bases in one comparison is how a lot of public argument on this topic goes wrong.

Put the publishing states together and the threshold that matters is this: at least four states now exceed $1 billion a year in data center exemption losses, Virginia, Texas, Ohio, and Georgia. A billion dollar annual line item gets a different kind of legislative attention than a $100 million one. That, more than any change in public mood, is what moved 2026.

The pause wave: Arizona, Illinois, Massachusetts, and the rest

The most useful way to read 2026 is by action taken, not by speeches given.

Paused. Arizona paused new applications for its Computer Data Center program from July 1, 2026 to June 30, 2029. Its program dates to 2013, asks for $50 million in Maricopa or Pima County and $25 million elsewhere, and normally runs 10 years. Approved centers are grandfathered, and the pause was estimated to save about $12 million in its first year, a small first year figure that says more about timing than about the long run cost. Illinois stopped processing applications as of July 1, 2026, under a directive from Governor Pritzker for a proposed two year pause. Its program is one of the more demanding on paper: $250 million over 60 months, 20 jobs at 120% of the county median wage, and carbon neutral status or green building certification, for exemptions lasting up to 20 years. Existing agreements are honored. Massachusetts is the newest program and the fastest reversal. It created a 20 year exemption in November 2024 for centers of at least 100,000 square feet investing $50 million and supporting 100 jobs, covering equipment, software, electricity, and construction costs. In June 2026 the state said it would not accept applications until further notice while it evaluates the program. Ohio, covered above, belongs in this group too, as does New Jersey, which ended a newer credit program this year.

Repealed or narrowed. Between May and August 2026, Maine, Minnesota, and Nebraska repealed their data center tax breaks, while Pennsylvania, Delaware, and North Carolina narrowed eligibility or added conditions. North Carolina's July 2026 budget is a good example of narrowing rather than repeal: it eliminated the exemption on data center electricity, leaving the rest in place. That electricity piece alone had cost at least $20 million a year and was projected at at least $160 million a year once planned centers are built. Minnesota took the same electricity step in 2025.

Still expanding. Not every state moved the same way. Kansas only joined the list in 2025, and Kentucky broadened its exemption in 2025. A developer reading only the repeal headlines would miss that the map is splitting, not closing. Some states are deciding the cost is too high at any scale. Others, usually with cheaper land, available power, and fewer competing projects, are deciding the same cost buys them a market position they do not yet have.

What I take from this as an appraiser

Three things belong in an underwriting file now that did not belong there five years ago.

First, treat an incentive as a term with an expiration and a political risk, not as a permanent feature of the market. That is the same discipline I use in the checklist I apply to underwrite a data center: identify the benefit, identify who grants it, and identify what ends it. A 20 year exemption in a state that paused applications in year two is worth less than its face value, and a valuation that capitalizes it at full value for the full term should say so openly.

Second, separate the bases before you compare states. State only versus state plus local, equipment only versus equipment plus electricity, and a published audit figure versus a projection all produce different numbers for the same program. The Georgia figures above differ by basis, not by honesty. In land valuation, where I already argue value should be read per MW rather than per acre, the same rule applies to incentives: normalize the unit before you rank the states.

Third, watch the grandfathering language more closely than the headline. In Arizona, Illinois, Ohio, and Massachusetts, existing agreements were honored or approved projects were grandfathered. The projects that lose in a pause are usually the next projects, the ones still assembling land and power. That raises the value of sites that are already entitled and already inside a program, and it raises the risk on speculative land priced as if the incentive available today will be available on the same terms at closing two years from now. That is a real, priceable difference, and it is where this policy wave will show up first in land markets.

The bottom line

The 2026 scorecard is not that states turned against data centers. It is that the bill became visible. Thirty eight states offer these incentives, at least four are now losing more than $1 billion a year to them, several of the largest programs cost roughly ten times what they were projected to cost, and in a single summer three states repealed, five paused, and three narrowed. Programs with published audits, clear sunsets, and honest cost reporting are in a stronger position than programs that grew quietly. For a landowner, a developer, or an investor, the practical change is simple. An incentive is no longer background. It is an underwriting input with a date, a basis, and a risk of its own, and in 2026 the states themselves started treating it that way.
