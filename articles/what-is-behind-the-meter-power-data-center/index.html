What Is Behind-the-Meter Power for a Data Center, and What It Changes in Underwriting

November 3, 2026

Every data center site I look at starts with the same question, and it is not about the land. It is about power: how much, from where, and by when. For most commercial buildings, that question has a boring answer. The utility delivers electricity to a meter, the building uses what passes through it, and the bill settles the rest. Behind-the-meter power breaks that pattern, and it is showing up in enough deals now that anyone valuing data center land needs a plain definition and a clear view of what it changes.

Behind the meter simply means power that is generated on the site, or dedicated to the site, and consumed before it crosses the utility meter in the ordinary way. Instead of waiting in line for a grid connection sized for the full load, the operator puts generation next to the load: gas turbines or engines on the pad, a dedicated plant built for the campus, or in the largest cases, a nuclear arrangement tied to one customer. The data center still may keep a grid connection for backup or supplemental supply. What changes is who controls the primary source, how fast it can be built, and who carries the risk when it does not run.

Why operators started building their own power

The honest answer is time. In the PJM footprint, the Congressional Research Service summary I work from counted 60 GW of potential large load projects in planning, with only 13 GW in advanced stages, and a utility estimate that if all of them came online there would be a 6 GW generation shortfall. Looked at nationally, meeting end of decade demand was estimated to require 50 GW to 60 GW of additional generation capacity. Data centers, meanwhile, account for 55% of all U.S. electricity demand growth, according to the industry report in my source set. A developer who waits for the normal queue is not just waiting for a wire. He is waiting for generation that in many places has not been built yet.

That is the gap behind-the-meter projects are trying to jump. A grid interconnection study can take years before construction even starts. A turbine package ordered by the developer, on land the developer controls, follows the developer's schedule instead of the utility's. I wrote in the powered land piece that dirt is now priced by the megawatt, and this is the other side of that same fact. If power is the scarce input, the party who can produce power on his own timeline holds the schedule, and the schedule is worth money.

This is no longer a fringe structure

AFCOM's 2026 State of the Data Center report found that 25% of sites now have on-site power generation, up from 19% the year before, and another 23% plan to add it. One site in four is not a pilot program. It is a standard option in site selection, and it belongs in the same first pass of questions as substation distance and fiber routes.

The examples in the Congressional Research Service record show the range. On the gas side, the Stargate site in Abilene, Texas, planned 360 MW of new on-site natural gas turbines. Homer City, Pennsylvania, planned 4.5 GW of on-site gas turbines with new gas infrastructure. Crusoe and Tallgrass in Cheyenne, Wyoming, planned 1.8 GW of on-site natural gas generation, and Meta's project in Richland Parish, Louisiana, involved 2.3 GW of new natural gas turbines built with Entergy. On the nuclear side, Meta issued a request for proposals in December 2024 for 1 GW to 4 GW of new nuclear generation. The Three Mile Island restart in Pennsylvania would return 800 MW to serve data centers, Google and NextEra plan to restart the 616 MW Duane Arnold plant in Iowa, and one Texas site in Carson County was listed as planning 11 GW of on-site nuclear generation.

Read those figures the way an appraiser should. Most of them are plans, agreements, and restarts reported in 2024 and 2025, not operating plants I can inspect today. The CRS list is a record of what was announced and underway, and announced capacity has a habit of shrinking, slipping, or changing fuel before it produces a single megawatt hour. The direction is real. The exact gigawatts should be treated as pipeline, not as supply.

What it changes in underwriting

First, the asset changes. A grid powered data center is a building with a utility contract. A behind-the-meter campus is a building plus a power plant, and the power plant has its own life, its own maintenance cycle, its own fuel supply, and its own permits. When I work through the checklist I use to underwrite a data center, the power section now has to ask who owns the generation, who operates it, what happens at the end of the fuel or service contract, and what the generation is worth, or what it costs to remove, if the tenant leaves. Those are not questions a standard industrial appraisal ever had to ask.

Second, the risk moves rather than disappears. Grid power carries queue risk and capacity price risk, as the electric bill research on this site shows. Behind-the-meter power trades those for fuel price risk, air permit risk, equipment lead time risk, and operating risk. Gas on site still needs a gas supply that holds up in a cold snap. Turbines still need maintenance and staff who know how to run them, in an industry where finding qualified operators is already hard. A valuation that treats on-site generation as a simple upgrade, with no offset for the risks it takes on, is only reading half the trade.

Third, speed has a price that cuts both ways in land value. A site that can support on-site generation, with room for turbines, fuel access, and a permitting path, can be worth more than a similar site that has to wait for the grid, and the $/MW method I use for data center land is the right way to see that difference, because the premium attaches to deliverable megawatts, not to acres. But the premium belongs to the specific plan that was permitted and built. Speculative land priced as if any buyer can simply drop in generation and skip the queue is priced on an assumption, and air permits, fuel interconnection, and neighbor opposition have ended more than a few of those assumptions. The construction cost figures matter here too: generation is one more large system on top of a build that already runs into the tens of millions of dollars per MW all in, and it has to be carried in the budget, not wished into it.

What behind the meter does not solve

It does not solve water, staffing, or community consent, and it does not always solve reliability. Many behind-the-meter designs still want a grid connection behind them, which means the queue does not vanish from the project. It shrinks to a smaller ask. It also does not settle the public argument about rates. A plant built for one customer keeps that customer's load off some shared costs, but fuel infrastructure, emissions, and any shared grid upgrades still land somewhere, and local officials are learning to ask where.

There is also an evidence gap I want to state plainly. The public record is good on announcements and poor on operations. I can cite how many sites report having on-site generation, and I can list the large projects by name and size. I cannot find a public source that reports, in one place, how much behind-the-meter capacity is actually operating today, at what capacity factor, and at what delivered cost per megawatt hour compared with grid supply. Until that exists, anyone who tells you behind-the-meter power is simply cheaper, rather than faster and more controllable, is getting ahead of the evidence.

The bottom line

Behind-the-meter power means the data center makes, or dedicates, its own electricity on site instead of relying entirely on what the utility delivers through the meter. A quarter of sites already have on-site generation, the largest operators are signing gas and nuclear deals measured in gigawatts, and the reason is the queue: the grid cannot add supply as fast as the load is arriving. For valuation, the structure is neither a gimmick nor a free pass. It moves value toward sites and operators who can deliver power on their own schedule, and it moves risk onto the project in the form of fuel, permits, and a power plant that has to be run and eventually dealt with. Price the speed. Price the risk too. An appraisal that only does one of those is not finished.

Sources: AFCOM, State of the Data Center 2026; Congressional Research Service, Data Center Energy Infrastructure: Federal Permit Requirements (2025); IBISWorld, Hyperscale Data Center Services in the US, Industry Report OD6584 (March 2026); U.S. data center energy consumption study (LBNL and DOE data).
